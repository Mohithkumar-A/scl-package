Backend mathematics · step-by-step

How does the backend fill damaged pixels?

The backend treats marked pixels as unknown variables. It defines a matrix-free least-squares problem whose equivalent normal equations say “be smooth with your neighbors, respect the visible boundary, and stay near a local color prior,” then solves it separately for each color channel.

UnknownsOnly pixels marked white in the mask are solved for.
ModelA 4-neighbor graph Laplacian plus a positive Tikhonov term, solved matrix-free with LSQR.
GuaranteePositive lambda makes the system symmetric positive definite.
The whole idea

Turn an image repair into a small mathematical problem

An image is a grid of intensity values. Most of those values are already known and must remain unchanged. The mask identifies the small set that is missing or damaged. The backend solves for just that set, using the intact pixels around it as fixed boundary conditions.

1. Decode the image into one or more numeric channels; decode the mask into known and unknown pixels.
↓
2. Index unknown pixels and their 4-connected neighbors.
↓
3. Assemble smoothness, boundary, and prior residuals as a matrix-free least-squares operator.
↓
4. Solve least squares LSQR refines the prior until the objective's optimality residual is small.
↓
5. Write back the solution only at masked coordinates; keep every other pixel and the alpha channel intact.
Key distinction: this is not a neural network and it does not search for semantic objects. It computes a smooth, mathematically constrained fill from the local image values around the damaged region.

The equations below are shown for one grayscale channel. RGB images repeat the same solve independently for red, green, and blue.

Step 1 · Define what is unknown

The binary mask partitions the image

Let the image domain be \(\mathcal D\), a set of row-column pixel coordinates. The decoded mask uses the convention \(1=\text{damaged}\) and \(0=\text{preserve}\). It divides the domain into:

\[ \Omega = \{p\in\mathcal D : M_p=1\}\quad\text{(damaged / unknown pixels)}, \qquad \mathcal K = \mathcal D\setminus\Omega\quad\text{(known / preserved pixels)}. \]

What this notation means: \(\Omega\) (capital Omega) is the set of all pixel coordinates where the mask is white (\(M_p=1\)). \(\mathcal K\) is the healthy remainder of the photograph. For every known pixel \(q\in\mathcal K\), its color \(I_q\) is fixed boundary data. For every damaged pixel \(p\in\Omega\), its intensity \(x_p\) is an unknown variable to solve for.

Mask threshold

The mask is converted to grayscale; values greater than 127 become damaged/unknown (\(M_p=1\)), and values at or below 127 are preserved (\(M_p=0\)).

Boundary requirement

Each 8-connected damage region must touch at least one known pixel. A fully masked image has no boundary information, so the backend rejects it.

Why preserve the mask exactly? It defines both the unknown vector and where the final solution is written. A display overlay can be resized for viewing, but the submitted mask has the original image dimensions.

Implementation: backend/app/utils/mask_io.py and backend/app/calculations/mask_validation.py.

Step 2 · Build a graph and index the unknowns

Convert the 2D damaged area into a vector

A pixel interacts with its valid horizontal and vertical neighbors. These are its 4-connected neighbors:

\[ \mathcal N_4(p)=\{p+(-1,0),\,p+(1,0),\,p+(0,-1),\,p+(0,1)\}\cap\mathcal D. \]

Plain language: \(\mathcal N_4(p)\) simply means the 4 immediate neighbors of pixel \(p\) (Up, Down, Left, Right) that lie inside the image boundaries.

Let \(d_p=|\mathcal N_4(p)|\), the number of valid neighbors (usually 2–4). Rather than dealing with a 2D table of thousands of rows and columns, we give each damaged pixel a simple integer ID from \(0\) to \(N-1\) (where \(N=|\Omega|\) is the total damaged pixel count). The unknown pixel values become a single vector: \[ x = \begin{bmatrix} x_0 \\ x_1 \\ \vdots \\ x_{N-1} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^N. \]

A single row of the grid graph
\[ \begin{array}{c|ccccc} \text{pixel} & \boxed{K} & \boxed{U} & \boxed{U} & \boxed{K} & \boxed{K}\\ \hline \text{role} & \text{fixed} & x_i & x_j & \text{fixed} & \text{fixed} \end{array} \]

The numerical smoothness graph is 4-connected. Separately, region validation and the boundary-color propagation use 8-connectivity, so diagonally touching damaged pixels count as one region.

Why not solve every image pixel? Known pixels are fixed data, not variables. Solving only the \(N\) damaged values saves work and guarantees that untouched pixels do not drift numerically.

Implementation: backend/app/calculations/difference_operators.py.

Step 3 · Choose the mathematical objective

Make neighboring pixels smooth while respecting what is known

The repair minimizes an energy with three parts: differences between adjacent unknown pixels, differences between unknown pixels and fixed known neighbors, and a Tikhonov penalty that keeps each solution value near a local prior \(x_p^{(0)}\).

Discrete energy for one channel \[ E(x)= \underbrace{\frac12\sum_{\substack{\{p,q\}\subset\Omega\\p\sim q}}(x_p-x_q)^2}_{\text{Term 1: Internal Smoothness}} +\underbrace{\frac12\sum_{\substack{p\in\Omega,\,q\in\mathcal K\\p\sim q}}(x_p-I_q)^2}_{\text{Term 2: Boundary Match to Photo}} +\underbrace{\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{p\in\Omega}(x_p-x_p^{(0)})^2}_{\text{Term 3: Tikhonov Prior Anchor}}. \]

What each term does in simple terms:

  • Term 1 (Smoothness): Adds an error penalty if two neighboring damaged pixels have different colors. Encourages seamless gradients.
  • Term 2 (Boundary Matching): Adds an error penalty if a damaged pixel on the edge doesn't match the healthy photo pixel right next to it.
  • Term 3 (Tikhonov Anchor): Multiplied by weight \(\lambda > 0\), it keeps each unknown near a local prior \(x_p^{(0)}\). Boundary pixels use the mean of known 4-neighbors; interior pixels use a propagated nearby known color.

The same energy is a least-squares objective

Each squared difference is a residual. Let \(e_p\) be the coordinate vector for damaged pixel \(p\). Stack the residual rows into a matrix \(C\), and their target values into a vector \(d\):

\[ \begin{array}{c|c} \text{constraint} & \text{residual}\\ \hline \text{unknown neighbors }p,q & (e_p-e_q)^T x\\ \text{known boundary pixel }q & e_p^T x-I_q\\ \text{prior at }p & \sqrt{\lambda}e_p^T x-\sqrt{\lambda}x_p^{(0)} \end{array} \] \[ E(x)=\frac12\|Cx-d\|_2^2. \]

This is Tikhonov-regularized least squares: least squares minimizes the sum of squared residuals, and weighting the prior residual by \(\sqrt{\lambda}\) gives the Tikhonov penalty. The backend applies the equivalent neighbor equations directly; it does not explicitly store \(C\) or multiply \(C^TC\).

The known image pixels are fixed exactly. The boundary residuals are soft penalties on adjacent unknowns; they do not force one unknown pixel to equal all of its known neighbors.

  1. Unknown-to-unknown smoothness: neighboring damaged pixels are encouraged to have similar intensity.
  2. Boundary fit: the damaged area is tied to the actual surrounding known pixels.
  3. Regularization: \(\lambda>0\) keeps the solution near a local color prior and makes the system better conditioned.

How does this code choose \(x_p^{(0)}\)?

If \(p\) has one or more known 4-neighbors, the prior is their average intensity. If it lies inside the damaged region and has no known 4-neighbor, the backend uses a nearby known boundary color propagated inward by an 8-connected breadth-first traversal. The same prior is also used as the initial solver guess.

Small \(\lambda\)

The discrete harmonic boundary fit has more influence; the prior pulls less strongly.

Larger \(\lambda\)

The solution is pulled more strongly toward its local prior. Extremely large values can make the fill look more like that prior.

What “Tikhonov” means here: the code does not call an opaque image-repair library. It defines a matrix-free residual operator whose equivalent normal equations have the graph-Laplacian-plus-λI form, then solves the least-squares problem iteratively.

The UI currently submits \(\lambda=0.01\); the backend validates the request value against its configured minimum and maximum.

Step 4 · Derive the least-squares system

Normal equations produce \(A x=b\)

For \(E(x)=\frac12\|Cx-d\|_2^2\), setting the gradient to zero gives the least-squares normal equations \(C^T(Cx-d)=0\), or \(C^TCx=C^Td\). Expanding this condition for one unknown pixel \(p\), split its 4-neighbors into unknown neighbors \(\mathcal N_U(p)\) and known neighbors \(\mathcal N_K(p)\):

\[ \frac{\partial E}{\partial x_p} =\sum_{q\in\mathcal N_U(p)}(x_p-x_q) +\sum_{q\in\mathcal N_K(p)}(x_p-I_q) +\lambda(x_p-x_p^{(0)})=0. \]

Collect the \(x_p\) terms. Since \(d_p=|\mathcal N_U(p)|+|\mathcal N_K(p)|\):

\[ (d_p+\lambda)x_p -\sum_{q\in\mathcal N_U(p)}x_q =\sum_{q\in\mathcal N_K(p)}I_q+\lambda x_p^{(0)}. \]

The normal equations are mathematically equivalent to this sparse linear system:

\[ A x=b,\qquad A_{ii}=d_i+\lambda,\qquad A_{ij}=\begin{cases}-1,&i\text{ and }j\text{ are 4-neighbor unknowns},\\0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases} \] \[ b_i=\sum_{q\in\mathcal N_K(i)}I_q+\lambda x_i^{(0)}. \]

Small example

If an interior unknown has three known neighbors with intensities \(80,100,120\) and one unknown neighbor \(x_j\), then:

\[ (4+\lambda)x_i-x_j=300+\lambda x_i^{(0)}. \]

The \(4\) is the total valid neighbor count. The unknown neighbor becomes a \(-1\) matrix entry; known intensities go on the right side.

The service does not assemble this normal matrix. It stores the damaged-to-damaged edge indices, boundary indices and values, and prior weights, then evaluates \(Cv\) and \(C^Tw\) as needed by LSQR. This avoids storing either a dense \(C\) or the normal matrix \(C^TC\).

Implementation: backend/app/calculations/tikhonov.py builds the residual operator; backend/app/calculations/iterative_solver.py applies LSQR.

Step 5 · Why this system is well behaved

The key theorem-level properties

1. Symmetric positive definiteness (SPD)

Write \(A=L_{\Omega\Omega}+\lambda I\), the unknown-region graph Laplacian block plus \(\lambda I\). For any nonzero vector \(z\):

\[ z^\mathsf T A z =\sum_{\substack{\{p,q\}\subset\Omega\\p\sim q}}(z_p-z_q)^2 +\sum_{\substack{p\in\Omega,\,q\in\mathcal K\\p\sim q}}z_p^2 +\lambda\sum_{p\in\Omega}z_p^2 >0. \]

Each term is nonnegative and the final term is strictly positive when \(z\ne0\), because \(\lambda>0\). Therefore \(A\) is symmetric positive definite: it is nonsingular and has only positive eigenvalues.

2. Strict diagonal dominance and Gershgorin

In row \(i\), the diagonal is \(d_i+\lambda\). The sum of absolute off-diagonal entries is the number of unknown neighbors. So the diagonal margin is:

\[ A_{ii}-\sum_{j\ne i}|A_{ij}| =|\mathcal N_K(i)|+\lambda>0. \]

The Gershgorin circle theorem places every eigenvalue in a disk centered at \(A_{ii}\) with radius equal to that off-diagonal sum. These disks lie strictly to the right of zero. Since \(A\) is symmetric, its eigenvalues are real and positive.

3. Nonsingular M-matrix and the discrete maximum principle

\(A\) has positive diagonal entries and nonpositive off-diagonal entries and is strictly diagonally dominant; it is a nonsingular M-matrix. Its inverse is entrywise nonnegative. With known intensities and priors in \([0,255]\), the ideal real-valued solution stays in that range. The image encoder still clips and rounds to valid 8-bit values as a final guard.

Why these matter: SPD gives a unique minimizer, strict diagonal dominance prevents a singular system, and the M-matrix structure supports stable, non-oscillatory propagation of boundary intensities.
Step 6 · Solve the least-squares problem

Matrix-free LSQR minimizes the residual sum

The backend applies LSQR, an iterative least-squares algorithm based on Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization. It starts from the local prior estimate and refines the unknown pixel vector to minimize \(\|Cx-d\|_2^2\). The smoothness, boundary, and Tikhonov residuals are evaluated directly from the grid-neighbor arrays; the potentially large matrix \(C\) is never built as a dense array.

\[ \text{LSQR uses repeated products } Cv \text{ and } C^T w \text{ to find } x^*=\operatorname*{argmin}_x\|Cx-d\|_2. \]
  1. Start: use the local boundary prior as the initial estimate.
  2. Build directions: bidiagonalization alternates products with \(C\) and \(C^T\), without forming \(C^TC\).
  3. Refine: update the pixel estimate to reduce the least-squares objective.
  4. Stop: check the normal-equation optimality residual every five iterations, up to the configured limit.
Why LSQR? It solves the least-squares objective directly and avoids explicitly forming normal equations, which would square the system's condition number numerically.

The reconstruction service uses matrix-free LSQR. Implementation: backend/app/calculations/iterative_solver.py and backend/app/services/reconstruction_service.py.

Step 7 · Measure least-squares optimality

The normal residual measures whether the objective has been minimized

Least squares can have a nonzero data residual at its optimum. The stationarity condition is instead:

\[ C^T(Cx-d)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad C^T(d-Cx)=0. \]

The backend normalizes the Euclidean length of this normal-equation residual:

\[ \operatorname{rel\_optimality} =\frac{\lVert C^T(d-Cx)\rVert_2}{\lVert C^Td\rVert_2+10^{-12}}. \]
  • The solver checks this optimality residual every 5 iterations and on the last permitted iteration.
  • Default stopping settings are tolerance \(10^{-4}\) and at most 500 iterations; both can be configured on the backend.
  • After clipping the solution to the valid intensity range, the solver recomputes the optimality residual for the values it will return. The response's converged flag is based on that final residual being below tolerance.
  • The service solves each color channel separately and reports the largest iteration count and final residual across channels.
  • Non-finite iterates trigger a numerical-instability error.
Residual is not image-quality truth. It says whether the computed vector solves this model's equations. It does not say that the result matches the unknown original photograph. That is why real backend responses leave PSNR and SSIM null without a clean reference image.
Step 8 · Reconstruct the image

Only the damaged coordinates are replaced

The backend starts the output channel array as a copy of the decoded input. After solving one channel, it inserts \(x_i\) at the coordinate associated with unknown index \(i\). It repeats this for each color channel.

\[ I_{\mathrm{out}}(p)= \begin{cases} x_p,&p\in\Omega,\\ I_{\mathrm{in}}(p),&p\in\mathcal K. \end{cases} \]

Therefore preserved RGB/grayscale samples are copied without being recomputed. If the original image has alpha transparency, that alpha array is carried through unchanged. At encoding, channel values are clipped to \([0,255]\), rounded, and written as PNG.

What the method is good at

Small holes, scratches, and smooth transitions where local continuation from nearby pixels is a reasonable model.

Its limitation

It does not infer object semantics or recover texture that is not encoded in nearby boundary values. Large holes and sharp patterns can look overly smooth.

One-sentence viva answer: “We treat masked pixels as unknowns, minimize the sum of squared smoothness, boundary, and Tikhonov-prior residuals using matrix-free LSQR, and write the solved values only into the mask.”
